Random draft:

I've been reading through Dr. Sara Stein Lubrano's best-selling book [*Don't Talk About Politics: How to Change 21st-Century Minds*](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1399413929?lv=shuf&channelId=500&plpRedirect=mhFallback), which takes a firm "debate is a waste of time" position. Some of her chapters are literally called "Don't Debate It" and "Don't Enter the 'Market' of Ideas." She's not alone in that take. I've seen so many people on so many platforms around the world mock debate, mock the idea of engaging in the marketplace of ideas. They say you won't change anyone's minds, that all it will cause is stress and frustration. Dr. Lubrano states that She claims that "debate and the 'market place of ideas' some of the fracturing of our social world" (pg. 234 of her book).

And to this I say, TRUE. And online debate in social media comments is probably too toxic and inconsequential to really be worth it. 

But I feel the one thing that all these detractors miss is that debate is NOT about changing minds in this day and age. Truth be told, I find a lot of the people who think "debate is a waste of time" are often self-proclaimed intellectuals who don't like the *boorishness* of online debate content. It reminds me of the people who are always excited when two individuals just sit down to "have a conversation," and that somehow this is more persuasive (note, Dr. Lubrano doesn't take this position, but I know a lot of people's podcasts she's gone on do).

However, everyone who engages in political debate for a living knows they are not going to convince their opponent the majority of the time. They probably even admit that most of the audience that supports either side will come and go with the same perspectives they had before. Maybe you can convince people on the margin (which is incidental to the main point), but the debaters understand the importance of this debate content: **branding**. 

Liberalism needs a brand. Many people take the liberal brand for granted, and I think we can see the consequences of where this has left off. Contrary to what detractors might say, media that have liberals dunking on idiots on the right helps the brand. If they deny this, then they deny the sheer effectiveness that figures on the right, like Ben Shapiro, Charlie Kirk, and Jordan Peterson, built their whole careers on. They managed to convince people, flirting with conservative thoughts, that conservatism is cool. Liberals are a bunch of SJW soyjacks. They largely didn't persuade through reason, they influenced via the aesthetics of the conservative "brand." They made conservatism seem cool. This is so obvious to those on the right that their entire media ecosystem is almost completely dedicated to this. It always surprises me that many people on the left don't want to engage with this idea on that level.

You might question why you need to advertise the political ideal of liberalism that everyone already knows. We all grew up learning about liberal values and virtues. Hearing how great democracy is, and why liberal figures are based. Why do we need to talk about it and debate it? Well, we need to do this for the same reason Coca-Cola spends [$4 billion dollars in marketing a year](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/markets/081315/look-cocacolas-advertising-expenses.asp). It's not to convince people to buy a Coke over a Pepsi, but it's to promote brand value and reduce **buyer's remorse**. 

Imagine if Coke didn't advertise itself. If you bought a Coke, sure, you might enjoy it, but you see so many other soda brands that are appealing and signal their status and appeal. You might second-guess why you bought the Coke, and maybe next time you might try a Pepsi. The ads promote brand loyalty by convincing people they made the right decision by joining the right club. 

This goes for many luxury brands. Everyone knows the luxury of a BMW, but BMW doesn't make ads to convince people to buy it. They make ads to reassure buyers that they didn't make a mistake getting a BMW when they could've gotten a Ford or other consumer sedan for 40% less. 

This type of branding applies to political ideology, too. If you subscribe to liberalism and its ideals but all you see around you is just media of liberals getting dunked on, getting made fun of, and being portrayed as "soy", then many people are going to have second guesses about their commitments. If there is nothing trying to defend the brand of liberalism by just advertising itself as something worthy to be defended, then of course, people are going to second-guess the quality of the product. 

Like, imagine if Pepsi ran ad after ad bashing Coke, calling it piss water or crude oil run off. Then imagine Coke didn't advertise itself at all as something worth enjoying. You would be putting *a lot* of trust in Coke's implicit branding to withstand constant public humiliation. I think many people mistakenly believed that liberalism could withstand those attacks, and detractors of public debate are making the same mistake. 

Debate and debate content serve as a form of advertisement. It doesn't even matter if it is online debate content or just congressmen trying to clip farm in congressional hearings. It all serves a similar purpose. Sure, it's propaganda, but propaganda is necessary for a reason. People mistake propaganda as manipulation of the masses. Sure, some of it lies, I'm not denying it, but that misses the real point. Propaganda promotes brand loyalty. It helps promote your brand as cool and worth staying in. Why do we, liberals, enjoy cool and based liberal propaganda? It's not because we are being manipulated, but it is because it reassures us that our side is based and not to completely check out of politics all together.

Dr. Sara Stein Lubrano's case in her book (overly generalizing it greatly here) is that you convince people of your political ideal by working with people in person. Getting offline, establishing communities, and making real differences. This changes people's minds, not an "ideal of the marketplace of ideas." She assumes that internet radicalization is because people's social networks are so fractured, and that establishing communities helps constrain their lives so that reactionary ideas aren't as persuasive (pg. 222 of hr book). If you bridge that gap, you can engage people who have felt disinterested in politics, and then you can actually make real change.

That all might be well and true, but this assumes A) people are actually going to stop looking at their screens and be influenced of political branding even if they get friends, and B) there are not enemies trying to compete against you in the same communities. Key in on her use of *persuasive*. Again, Coke's marketing isn't trying to persuade non-political people to your side. Coke is trying to build loyalty by lowering regret and keeping people from *leaving* its side. 

There's a bit of both going on with the politically disaffected, but to ignore the impact on reaffirming brand loyalty is a mistake. Having a quality end product is good and important and can keep people coming back, but you would be foolish to assume your political product can uphold its values purely through grassroots cult followings. This is the threat of the competitor. You *need* to play the game of mass marketing. Even if liberals get to the same point of conservatives in having offline community engagement, liberals would still need to fight the buyers remorse. Because the conservative side is great at engaging its dissatisfied people, the liberals will still face the dilemma of keeping people reassured. Plus, strong self-branding makes the other side not sure of *their* brand.

Imagine if no doctors or figures stood up and debated vaccines publicly during COVID-19. What do you have? You have a massive amount of media bashing in medical institutions as lame. Here, the target audience of the pro-vaccine debaters is NOT undecided people on whether or not to get vaccinated. It's to instill loyalty to those who did get vaccinated and to make sure they don't regret their choice. Because if everyone saying the vaccine doesn't work, they are putting a lot of faith on science they don't fully understand. And if no scientist or public figure is defending these ideas, then that doesn't really inspire confidence.

Sure, you have a cool community of liberals, but look over there. The conservative community also looks like its having fun, and they actually are filled with cool bro shit and not soy cuck shit like the libs. Don't you feel like you made the wrong decision? This is what debate media helps to prevent. 

---

TL;DR Coke doesn't spend billions of dollars advertising itself to convince people to drink it. It spends that much money to promote its brand in order to reassure consumers that they made the right choice, instilling customer loyalty. Many luxury products do this: use advertising to mitigate consumer remorse.  The core mistake many detractors of debate make is mistaking its purpose. They assume that the purpose is to convince people onto your side by proving your side correct in the marketplace of ideas, but the real purpose is to reassure your own side that you are sticking with the right team. In the world of fast-paced media, you need this angle or else you put too much faith that the quality of the product will speak for itself against an onslaught of detractors. Imagine if Coke didn't advertise itself in the face of endless ad campaigns from Pepsi absolutely bashing it? There's gonna be a lot of Coke fans who would be second-guessing their loyalties. This would effectively be trying to promote liberalism without defending its ideals against enemies. 

While promoting communities and real-world consequences of liberalism is good as an end product, for the same reason, Coke needs to be tasty to sell well, you have to reassure people they are making the right choice. You can only achieve so much of this while never engaging with the other side.

You cannot subsist on ONLY real-world communities and impact if no one is going to be up to bat defending it against attack. Be like Coke. Understand your product's value and why it deserves continuous promotion. 

There's more to this theory I'll probably keep fleshing out and expanding on as I go over this book and other material again. Will probably write something much longer up, but I am, once again, blown away by anyone's ability to understand and actively want to engage with the media system we live in. If the other side has a winning strategy, I am not convinced we will come up with a better strategy by not dealing with "debate" simply because it doesn't appeal to our sensibilities.
