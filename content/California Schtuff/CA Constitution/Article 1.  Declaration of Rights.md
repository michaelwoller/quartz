# Preamble

"We, the People of the State of California, grateful to Almighty God for our freedom, in order to secure and perpetuate its blessings, do establish this Constitution."
- no stated purpose
- original was in 1849 vs current 1979

# Article I

- 32 sections
- was 24 sections in the original 1879 version
- Prop 7 (1974) reorginaized this and a lot of individual initaitive amendments on top
	- unlike federal bill of rights, this is very flexible and has different langauges from over 100 years of editing

## §1 — Inalienable rights (life, liberty, property, safety, happiness, privacy) ★★★
- inalienable rights: 
	- life
	- liberaty
	-  property
	- safety
	- hapinesss
	- privacy (added in 1972; _White v. Davis_, *Hill v. NCAA*)
		- Driven by concern over government data-gathering and surveillance in the emerging computer age 
			- - Voters approved it 62.9%–37.1%
		- _White v. Davis_ (1975)
			- UCLA students suing over covert police infiltration of campus political groups
			- first major interpretation, read the clause as requiring a "compelling public need" test before government could intrude on privacy
		- *Committee to Defend Reproductive Rights v. Myers* (1981) 
			- state privacy right used to strike down Medi-Cal abortion funding restrictions, a result _Roe_ alone wouldn't have compelled
		- _Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Association_ (1994)
			- challenge by college athletes to random drug testing
			- abandoned the compelling-interest standard for privacy claims against private parties, folded the right back toward common-law tort doctrine
### §1.1 — Reproductive freedom ★★
- Reproductive freedom (added Nov 2022) by **Proposition 1**,
- in response to *Dobbs (2022)* overturning Roe v Wade
- gurantees right to abortion and to choose or refuse contraceptives
- untesteed
## **§2 — Free speech and press ★★★**
- gives other basic rights like speach, writing publish
	- more specific than 1st amendment
	- _runeyard Shopping Center v. Robins_ (1979) held that California's free speech clause protects leafletting and petitioning inside private shopping malls
		- something that was ruled that isn't federally guaranteed
## **§3 — Petition, assembly, and public records ★★**
- freedom of assembly/petition
	- plus a "right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business" added by Proposition 59 (2004)
- California Sunshine Law constitutionalization requiring open meetings and records and instructs courts to construe access statutes braodly
- requires transparency

## **§4 — Free exercise and no establishment of religion ★★**
- similar to bill of rights

## §5 — Military subordinate to civil power ★
- unlitigated in modern era, anachronistic

## **§6 — Slavery and involuntary servitude prohibited, except to punish crime ★★**
- same "except to punish crime" as 13th amendment
	- prison-labor loophole
	- Prop 6 in 2024 wanted to strip this provision but failed
		- there was worry prisoner's would demand minimum wage

## **§7 — Due process and equal protection ★★★**
- CA equivalent to 14th Amendments equal protection clause
- voters in 1979 voted to cap the state courts' power to order pupil transportation remedies beyond what federal equal protection law would independently require
	- pulled back rights
- _Crawford v. Board of Education_ (1976) 
	- court-ordered school busing decision

### **§7.5 — Marriage ★★★**
-  Proposition 8 (November 2008) added Section 7.5: "Only marriage between a man and a woman is valid or recognized in California"
	- passed 52.3%–47.7%
- _In re Marriage Cases_ (May 15, 2008)
	- California Supreme Court held, under §1 and §7 (privacy and equal protection), that same-sex couples had a fundamental right to marry
- Struck down a statutory ban voters had themselves enacted in 2000 via Proposition 22
-  _Strauss v. Horton_ (2009) 
	- upheld Prop 8 against state-constitutional challenge
	- held it was a valid _amendment_ rather than an impermissible _revision_
		- didn't retroactively invalidate the ~18,000 same-sex marriages performed in the interim window
- _Perry v. Schwarzenegger_ (N.D. Cal., 2010) 
	- invalidated Section 7.5 on **federal** constitutional grounds
		- boo
		- made this clause present but void "zombie clause"
- _Obergefell v. Hodges_ (2015) 
	- effectively mooted the provision nationwide
- Proposition 3 (November 2024) formally repealed and replaced it (even though SCOTUS ruled same sex marraige was fine by that time)
	- Added affirmative "the right to marry is a fundamental right" clause tied explicitly to §1 privacy, due process, equal protection, and pursuit of safety and happiness
		- Passed ~62.6%–37.4%
		- cleared the Legislature unanimously (67–0 Assembly, 31–0 Senate) as ACA 5, introduced by Assemblymember Evan Low
## §8 — No discrimination in business, profession, or employment ★★
- Prohibits disqualifying a person from a trade or profession based on sex, race, creed, color, or national/ethnic origin
- Predates most federal employment-discrimination law in spirit
- Federal Title VII and California's own FEHA statute now do most of the practical work
- less independently litigated today

## §9 — Bill of attainder, ex post facto, contracts clause ★
- federal doctrine makes this not important

## §10 — Witnesses not unreasonably detained; no imprisonment for civil debt ★
- rooted in 19th century issues
- anachronistic

## §11 — Habeas corpus ★★
- Suspension permitted only for rebellion or invasion where public safety requires it
- Parallel to Article I, §9 of the federal Constitution
- Never actually invoked in California history
- Doctrinally significant as a backstop against detention without judicial review

### §12 — Bail ★★
- _In re Humphrey_ (Cal. Supreme Court, 2021) 
	- setting bail an arrestee can't afford, without considering ability to pay or exploring non-monetary release conditions, violates due process and equal protection
- Major cash-bail reform decision
- Came down on state constitutional grounds _after_ Proposition 25 (2020), a ballot measure to abolish cash bail outright, had failed at the polls
- Courts and direct democracy pulling in different directions on the same issue within about a year of each other

## §13 — Search and seizure ★★★
- For decades, California courts read this as providing _more_ protection than the federal Fourth Amendment
	- due to "independent state ground" doctrine
	- _People v. Brisendine_ (1975) 
		- crystallized this broader reading
- Came from Proposition 8 (June 1982, the original "Victims' Bill of Rights" 
	- a different measure from the same-numbered 2008 marriage proposition) added a "Truth-in-Evidence" provision
- Forced California courts to admit evidence the federal exclusionary rule would allow, eliminating the broader state-law suppression standard
- _In re Lance W._ (1985) 
	- upheld this provision
## §14 — Felony prosecution by indictment or information ★
-  Structural provision governing charging methods

### §14.1 — No post-indictment preliminary hearing if charged by grand jury indictment ★
- Technical criminal procedure

## §15 — Criminal defendant's rights: speedy/public trial, compulsory process, counsel, confrontation, double jeopardy, self-incrimination ★★★
- California's rough equivalent of the federal Fifth and Sixth Amendments bundled into a single section
- Enormous ordinary criminal-procedure litigation runs through here continuously
- Tends to track federal doctrine closely rather than break independent ground the way §2 or §13 have
## §16 — Jury trial (civil and criminal) ★★
- Trial by jury is an "inviolate right"
- California permits non-unanimous _civil_ verdicts (three-fourths of the jury may render a verdict)
	-  departure from federal civil practice
- Criminal juries must remain unanimous
- Allows reduced jury size by party agreement in lesser cases

## §17 — Cruel or unusual punishment; excessive fines ★★★
- Best case study in the article of direct democracy overriding the court
- Clause reads "cruel **or** unusual" (disjunctive), not "cruel **and** unusual" like the Eighth Amendment (conjunctive)
	- a deliberate distinction to provide more rights
		- _People v. Anderson_ (1972) 
			- used the disjunctive wording to strike down the death penalty statewide as unconstitutionally cruel even if not unusual
			- Commuted every pending death sentence in the state, including Charles Manson and Sirhan Sirhan
			- Voters overturned this within the year via Proposition 17 (November 1972), adding what's now Section 27
				- Declared capital punishment neither cruel nor unusual as a matter of constitutional text 

## §18 — Treason ★
- Narrowly defined (levying war against the state, adhering to its enemies)
- Essentially never invoked

## §19 — Eminent domain / takings ★★

- Broader than the federal Takings Clause on its face 
	- covers property "taken _or damaged_," not just taken outright
- Guarantees a jury determination of just compensation unless waived

## §20 — Noncitizens have the same property rights as citizens ★★

- Superseded California's Alien Land Law history 
	- 1913 and later statutes barring Japanese immigrants and other "aliens ineligible for citizenship" from owning land
- Only fully dismantled through court decisions and constitutional cleanup by the mid-20th century
- Rarely litigated today

## §21 — Separate property in marriage ★
- Defines property owned before marriage, or acquired during marriage by gift, will, or inheritance, as separate property
- Implemented mostly through the Family Code rather than directly litigated as a constitutional matter

## §22 — No property qualification for voting or holding office ★
- Anti-plutocratic holdover from 19th-century constitutional design
	- I've never heard of this kind of qualification so this is interesting 
- Uncontroversial and unlitigated

## §23 — Grand juries, at least one drawn and summoned per county annually ★
- Purely structural/administrative

## §24 — Independent state constitutional grounds ★★★
- rights guaranteed by the California Constitution "are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution"
	- aka, CA can give you more than what is stated in the federal constiution
	- basis of _Pruneyard_ (§2) and _Brisendine_ (§13)
	- California courts treat state rights as a floor, not a ceiling, tied to federal interpretation only where state courts choose to align
- Voters have used the initiative process to constrain when they disagreed with how far courts took this independence 
	- the 1982 Truth-in-Evidence provision under §13 being the clearest example
- The section that explains _why_ Article I keeps producing court/initiative feedback loops

## §25 — Right to fish on public lands and waters ★
-  individual right to recreational/subsistence fishing on public lands and waters, subject to state regulation

## §26 — Constitution is mandatory and prohibitory unless declared otherwise ★★
- Courts must treat constitutional text as self-executing and legally binding
## §27 — Death penalty preserved ★★★
- Direct legislative override of _People v. Anderson_, via Proposition 17 (1972) 
	- see §17 for the full sequence
- Declares the death penalty statutes in effect as of February 17, 1972 to remain in full force
- Declares capital punishment neither cruel nor unusual
- Subject to legislative amendment or repeal by ordinary statute

## §28 — Victims' rights / Marsy's Law ★★★
- Origin traces to the same 1982 Proposition 8 that added the Truth-in-Evidence provision to §13 
	- framed as a "Victims' Bill of Rights"
- Substantially expanded and renumbered by Proposition 9 (2008), branded "Marsy's Law"
	- Named after Marsalee "Marsy" Nicholas, a UC Santa Barbara student murdered by an ex-boyfriend in 1983
	- Her family, a week after the murder, were ecountered the accused at a grocery store unaware he'd been released on bail
- Enumerates victim rights: fairness, privacy, protection, notice, to be heard at proceedings
- Right to reasonable protection from the defendant
- Right to restitution
- Right to a speedy trial and prompt conclusion
- Extends "right to safety" language to school and college campuses
- Ongoing litigation over interaction with parole hearings, discovery obligations, and defendants' competing procedural rights

## §29 — People's right to due process and speedy/public trial in criminal cases ★★
- Balances §15's defendant-focused rights with an explicit statement that the prosecution/public also has due process and speedy-trial interests
- Part of the same 1980s–90s "victims' and public's rights" cluster as §28
- Reflects broader pushback against what proponents saw as an over-protective criminal procedure regime

## §30 — Joinder of criminal cases; hearsay at preliminary hearings ★★
- Confirms the Legislature or the People via initiative may permit joining multiple criminal charges in one proceeding
- Notably allows hearsay evidence (including officer-relayed hearsay) at preliminary hearings
	- controversial lowering of the evidentiary bar at the charging stage relative to ordinary federal practice

## §31 — Proposition 209 / ban on race- and sex-based preferences ★★★
- Added by Proposition 209 (1996)
- Prohibits the state from discriminating against or granting preferential treatment to any individual or group based on race, sex, color, ethnicity, or national origin
- Applies to public employment, public education, and public contracting
- Effectively bans affirmative action programs using those categories in state institutions
- Upheld against constitutional challenge in _Coalition for Economic Equity v. Wilson_ (9th Cir., 1997)
- Voters declined to repeal it in 2020 
	- Proposition 16 failed despite significant institutional and political support

## §32 — Public safety / parole and sentencing reforms from Proposition 57 ★★
- Added November 2016
- Restricts federal courts' ability to order early release of prisoners to relieve overcrowding without the state first getting a chance to implement its own rehabilitation and parole-consideration reforms
- Direct response to _Brown v. Plata_ (2011) 
	- U.S. Supreme Court decision ordering California to reduce its prison population because overcrowding produced unconstitutional conditions of confinement
- Expanded parole consideration for nonviolent offenders
- Expanded credit-earning opportunities for good behavior and rehabilitative program participation
- Most recent substantive addition to Article I
