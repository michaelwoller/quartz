- 20 sections (plus 2.5), covering the mechanics of direct democracy in California
- Added to the constitution in 1911 as part of the Progressive-era reform package pushed by Governor Hiram Johnson,
	- in direct response to concerns about corporate political control 
		- especially the Southern Pacific Railroad's dominance of the Legislature
- Substantially reorganized by Proposition 14 (June 8, 1976) as part of the broader 1960s–70s constitutional revision effort
- This is the article that explains _why_ Article I keeps producing court/initiative feedback loops 
- Hiram Johnson called the recall power the "gun behind the door"  

**Importance key:** 
- ★★★ heavily litigated / foundational/live flashpoint 
- ★★ real doctrinal weight, narrower reach 
- ★ largely settled, technical, or rarely invoked

### §1 — All political power inherent in the people ★★

- "Government is instituted for their protection, security, and benefit, and they have the right to alter or reform it when the public good may require"
- Foundational framing statement for the entire direct-democracy apparatus that follows
- Echoes §1 of Article I in spirit but here applied specifically to popular sovereignty over government structure rather than individual rights
- Rarely litigated on its own

### §2 — Voter eligibility ★★★

- U.S. citizen, 18 years of age, resident of California may vote
	- this is for STATE elections. Federal voter age wasn't 18 until the 70s
- Amended by Proposition 17 (November 2020), alongside §4, to restore voting rights to people on parole immediately upon completion of prison term
	- Before Prop 17 California was one of only three states requiring both prison _and_ parole completion before voting rights returned
- Prop 17 passed with roughly 60% approval; affected an estimated 50,000 Californians on parole
- Originated as Assembly Constitutional Amendment 6, authored by Assemblymember Kevin McCarty
- Framed by supporters as a rehabilitation and civic-reintegration measure
	- opponents argued it undermined accountability for serious/violent offenders
- Disproportionate impact data cited in the campaign:
	- in 2016, Black Californians were 26% of parolees but only 6% of the state's adult population
-  the original 1974 Proposition 10 first allowed restoration of voting rights after completion of sentence _and_ parole
	- Prop 17 is best understood as finishing that reform 46 years later

### §2.5 — Votes cast in accordance with law shall be counted ★
- Added by Proposition 43 (March 2002)
- Straightforward anti-disenfranchisement guarantee
	- a vote cast in compliance with state election law must be counted
- Rarely an independent basis for litigation
	- functions as a backstop principle

### §3 — Legislature defines residency, registration, and free elections ★
- Delegates administrative definition of residency and voter registration procedures to the Legislature
- Also guarantees "free elections" as a general principle
- Mostly implemented through the Elections Code rather than directly litigated as constitutional text

### §4 — Disqualification of electors ★★★
- Directs the Legislature to prohibit improper election practices and disqualify electors who are mentally incompetent or currently imprisoned for a felony
- Also amended by Proposition 17 (2020) 
	- removed "or on parole" from the disqualification list, which is the actual operative mechanism behind the §2 change above
- The two sections work as a pair: 
	- §4 tells the Legislature _who_ to disqualify
	- §2 states the underlying eligibility rule and restoration timing

### §5 — Voter-nominated ("top-two") primary ★★★
- jungle primary position
- Amended by Proposition 14 (June 2010), authored by then-State Senator Abel Maldonado as part of a deal to secure his vote for the 2009 state budget
- Based on a proposal originally drafted by the Independent Voter Project
- Replaced closed partisan primaries with a single ballot: all candidates for congressional and state office, regardless of party, run together, and the top two vote-getters advance to the general election
	- Passed with 53.7%–54% of the vote, did not apply to presidential primaries
- Predecessor effort, Proposition 62 (2004), had failed at 46%
- California's _earlier_ blanket primary system (different from top-two) had been struck down federally in _California Democratic Party v. Jones_ (2000) for forcing parties to associate with candidates they didn't choose
- Top-two survived because it doesn't purport to select a party's nominee at all 
	- voters aren't picking "the Democratic candidate," they're just picking two names to advance
- Validated in principle by _Washington State Grange v. Washington State Republican Party_ (2008, U.S. Supreme Court), upholding an essentially identical Washington State system
	- so scotus approved WA's system, indirectly approving CA
- California's own version was separately challenged in _Field v. Bowen_
	- upheld by a California appellate court in September 2011
- A second federal challenge argued top-two unconstitutionally burdens minor-party ballot access
	- also rejected, with courts holding voter participation rights outweigh party ballot-access claims
-  practical effect: general elections can now feature two candidates from the _same_ party (e.g., two Democrats)
	- locking out minor parties and independents from the general-election ballot entirely in many districts 
	- a frequent point of criticism from third parties

### §6 — Nonpartisan offices ★★
- All **judicial**, school, county, and city offices, including Superintendent of Public Instruction, are "nonpartisan"
	- i.e., not based on political party registration
		- judicial is key unique aspect here
- Also amended by Prop 14 (2010) 
	- parties cannot nominate candidates for these offices, and no party preference appears on the ballot for them
- Distinguishes California from states where judicial elections are openly partisan
### §7 — Secret ballot ★
- "Voting shall be secret": one sentence, self-explanatory
- Essentially unlitigated
- foundational but uncontroversial
	- well, at least state level, but MAGA fuck wits are trying to challenge these on a state by state level (probably not in CA though)

### §8 — Initiative power ★★★
- Defines ballot initiatives
	- a **ballot initiative** is a form of direct democracy that allows registered voters to propose new state laws or constitutional amendments.
		- By bypassing the Governor and the California State Legislature, citizens can place a proposed measure directly on the statewide ballot for a public vote
	-  Defined formally as the power of electors to propose statutes and constitutional amendments directly, bypassing the Legislature
- Signature thresholds tied to the total votes cast for governor in the most recent election (currently 8% for a constitutional amendment, 5% for a statute)
- Single-subject rule: an initiative addressing more than one subject area may not be submitted to voters or have effect
	- proponents draft broadly, opponents challenge the measure post-passage as covering multiple unrelated subjects to try to invalidate it
- the amendment-vs-revision distinction
	- an initiative can _amend_ the constitution via ordinary majority vote
	- a wholesale _revision_ requires the more demanding constitutional convention or legislative-referral process (Article XVIII)
- _Raven v. Deukmejian_ (1990)
	- the "Crime Victims Justice Reform Act" initiative would have required California courts to construe state criminal-defendant rights consistently with, and never more broadly than, federal constitutional law 
		- effectively neutering §24 of Article I
	- The California Supreme Court struck this off the ballot, holding it was an impermissible _revision_ (fundamentally altering the judiciary's constitutional role) rather than a permissible _amendment_
- Established that only California courts may control how California courts interpret the state constitution
	- federal courts can't touch it or state legislatures/executives
- One of the only real judicial limits on what the initiative process can accomplish

### §9 — Referendum power ★★
- Defines  referendum as the power of electors to approve or reject statutes (or parts of statutes) already enacted by the Legislature
- Cannot be used on urgency statutes, statutes calling elections, or tax-levy/appropriation statutes for ordinary state expenses
	- urgency statutes take effect immediately upon being signed by the Governor and chaptered by the Secretary of State
		- require a two-thirds vote in each house plus a statement of facts constituting the urgency
- Requires the same signature threshold as an initiative statute 
	- 5% of votes cast for governor
- Used far less often in practice than the initiative power

### §10 — Effective date, Attorney General review, conflicting measures ★★
- An initiative statute or referendum approved by a majority takes effect the day after the election unless it specifies otherwise
	- urgency statutes wouldn't, for example
	- Effective-date mechanics amended by Proposition 71 (June 2018) to **the fifth day after the Secretary of State certifies the results (roughly six weeks post-election)**
		- to avoide law taking effect before mail in vote ballots were fully counted
- Before circulation, a copy of any initiative or referendum petition must go to the Attorney General, who prepares an official title and summary
- If a referendum petition targets only part of a statute, the rest of the statute still takes effect on schedule
- Conflicting-measures rule: if two or more measures on the same ballot conflict, the one with the most affirmative votes prevails 
	- clarified in _Taxpayers to Limit Campaign Spending v. Fair Political Practices Commission_ (1990)
		- held that courts should apply this section-by-section rather than allow piecemeal combination of conflicting initiatives (referencing an unintended-consequences precedent from 1908 Oregon)
			- I'll leave it at that, probably not important

### §11 — Local initiative and referendum ★
- Extends initiative and referendum powers to electors of each city or county, under procedures set by the Legislature
- Does not affect charter cities' own separate initiative/referendum procedures
- Bars conditional or alternative-provision local initiatives (measures that would take effect differently depending on vote percentage)
	- a technical anti-gerrymandering-of-outcomes rule

### §12 — No naming individuals or private corporations ★★
- No constitutional amendment or initiative/legislative statute may name a specific individual to hold office, or name/identify a specific private corporation to perform a function or hold power
- A structural anti-corruption, anti-personalization safeguard 
- Rarely triggers litigation because it's rarely violated outright, but it shapes how initiatives are drafted (generic categories rather than named entities)

### §13 — Recall power defined ★★
- "Recall is the power of the electors to remove an elective officer"
- Part of the original 1911 Progressive-era package alongside initiative and referendum
- Sets up the mechanics detailed in §§14–19

### §14 — Recall petition and signature requirements ★★★
- Recall of a state officer initiated by delivering a petition to the Secretary of State alleging reason for recall
- Sufficiency of the stated reason is explicitly **not reviewable**
	- courts cannot second-guess whether the recall reason is "good enough"
- Proponents have 160 days to circulate and file signed petitions
	- Statewide officer: signatures equal to 12% of the last vote for that office, with signatures from each of 5 counties equal to at least 1% of that county's last vote for the office
	- Legislators, Board of Equalization members, and appellate/trial court judges: signatures equal to 20% of the last vote for the office
- 2021 Newsom recall: needed 1,495,709 verified signatures
	- proponents (led by Orrin Heatlie) met that threshold, extended by a COVID-related court order to March 17, 2021

### §15 — Recall election mechanics ★★★
- Election called by the Governor, held 60–80 days after signature certification
- Two-question ballot: (1) should the officer be recalled? (2) who should replace them if so?
- Amended by Proposition 183 (November 1994) 
	- added the provision that the targeted officer cannot be a candidate on the replacement question
- **2003 recall of Gray Davis**: only successful gubernatorial recall in California history
	- Davis received 44.6% support to remain
	- Arnold Schwarzenegger won the replacement question with 48.6% of a field of over 100 candidates
	- Davis himself sued in 2003 challenging the recall's constitutionality on equal-protection grounds (seeking to be listed as a replacement candidate)
		- the California Supreme Court's handling of that case became the key precedent cited in 2021
- **2021 Newsom recall**: Newsom retained his seat 61.9%–38.1% against a field of 46 replacement candidates
	- a federal lawsuit filed weeks before the election argued the two-question format violated "one person, one vote" (since a replacement candidate could win with far fewer votes than Newsom received on the retention question) 
		- aka, more people could have voted "don't recall newsom" (which lost) than people who voted for his replacement
			- effectively, the "don't recall newsom" would have been votes in favor or newsom
		- this challenge failed, consistent with the 2003 precedent, on the theory that the recall is legally two separate elections conducted on one ballot, not a single race
- A 2026 ballot measure has been proposed to restructure this process  eliminating the simultaneous successor election in favor of filling vacancies according to existing succession rules, and allowing a recalled officer to run again at a later special election (though not to be appointed to fill the vacancy directly)

### §16 — Legislature implements petition, candidate, and election procedures ★
- Directs the Legislature to establish the operational details:
	- circulation, filing, and certification of petitions
	- nomination of replacement candidates; conduct of the recall election itself
- Added by Proposition 14 (1976, not the 2010 top-two measure — a distinct proposition using the same number)
- Purely implementing/administrative

### §17 — Recall of Governor or Secretary of State — interim duties ★
- If a recall targets the Governor or Secretary of State specifically, that office's recall-administration duties shift to the Lieutenant Governor or Controller, respectively
### §18 — Reimbursement of a non-recalled officer's expenses ★
- If an officer survives a recall vote, the state reimburses their legally and personally incurred recall-election expenses
- Narrow, technical, rarely a source of dispute

### §19 — Recall of local officers ★
- Requires the Legislature to provide for recall of local officials
- Does not override charter cities/counties that have their own recall provisions written into their charters
### §20 — Terms of elective office ★
- Terms of offices established by the constitution (other than Legislature members) begin the Monday after January 1 following the election
- Added by Proposition 14 (1976)
- Purely calendrical/administrative
