- Short article, added wholesale to the constitution by Proposition 6 (November 7, 1972) as part of the 1960s–70s constitutional revision effort
- Officially 9 numbered sections (1–9), with §3.5 inserted as an add-on that doesn't count toward that base number

**Importance key:** ★★★ heavily litigated / foundational / live flashpoint — ★★ real doctrinal weight, narrower reach — ★ largely settled, technical, or rarely invoked

---
### §1 — California as an inseparable part of the United States ★

- "The State of California is an inseparable part of the United States of America, and the United States Constitution is the supreme law of the land"
- A textual acknowledgment of the federal Supremacy Clause, stated from the state's own side
- Essentially declaratory — restates a principle already established federally, so it carries little independent litigation weight
- Functions more as a framing statement than an operative rule

### §2 — Boundaries and capital ★

- State boundaries are those set in the original 1849 Constitution, as modified by subsequent statute
- Sacramento is the capital of California
- Purely definitional; no meaningful litigation history

### §3 — Separation of powers ★★★

- "The powers of state government are legislative, executive, and judicial. Persons charged with the exercise of one power may not exercise either of the others except as permitted by this Constitution"
- This is the textual anchor for California's separation-of-powers doctrine — notably, the **federal** Constitution contains no equivalent express separation-of-powers clause (it's inferred structurally from Articles I–III), so California's version is explicit where the federal model is implicit
- Directly ties to Article IV §1 (legislative power vested in the Legislature, subject to reserved initiative/referendum powers), Article V §1 (executive power vested in the Governor), and Article VI §1 (judicial power vested in the courts)
- In practice, the three branches routinely overlap in ways courts have upheld as permissible: the Legislature writes bills but the Governor can veto them; agencies write regulations (quasi-legislative) and adjudicate disputes (quasi-judicial) under legislative delegation
- The provision that gives §3.5 (below) its teeth — because §3.5 exists specifically to police the line between the judicial power to invalidate a statute and the _administrative_ branch's power to do the same thing

### §3.5 — Limits on administrative agency power over statutes ★★★

- Added by Proposition 5 (June 6, 1978)
- An administrative agency — including one created directly by the Constitution or by initiative statute — has **no power** to:
    - (a) declare a statute unenforceable, or refuse to enforce it, on constitutional grounds unless an appellate court has already made that determination
    - (b) declare a statute unconstitutional at all
    - (c) refuse to enforce a statute on the theory that federal law or federal regulations preempt it, unless an appellate court has already so ruled
- Functionally, this forces every constitutional or preemption objection through the courts rather than letting an agency simply decline to enforce a law it believes is invalid — a direct separation-of-powers safeguard reserving constitutional review exclusively to the judiciary
- Cited and applied in California tax and administrative adjudications (e.g., before the State Board of Equalization) to bar the Board from refusing to enforce a statute on constitutional grounds
- A quiet but consequential limit on the administrative state — it means California agencies, however expert or well-intentioned, cannot act as a shadow judiciary

### §4 — Salary protection for elected officers and judges ★★

- (a) Salaries of elected state officers cannot be reduced during their term of office; laws setting these salaries are treated as appropriations
- (b) Special judicial salary rule, effective January 1, 1981: a judge's base salary is pegged to the 1980 pay scale as if elected in 1978; the Legislature can increase (or halt future increases to) judicial salaries during a term, but can never cut a judge's pay below the highest level reached during that term
- Explicitly states that judicial salary laws do **not** count as a contractual obligation under Article I §9 (the contracts clause) — closing off a potential argument that salary freezes breach a contract
- A judicial-independence safeguard: prevents the political branches from punishing sitting judges financially for unpopular rulings
- Proposition 61 (1986), which would have altered related compensation/pension rules, was put to voters and **defeated**

### §5 — Suits against the state ★★

- "Suits may be brought against the State in such manner and in such courts as shall be directed by law"
- This is California's sovereign-immunity waiver provision — the state consents to be sued, but only through mechanisms the Legislature defines by statute (principally the California Government Claims Act)
- Real doctrinal weight in practice: nearly every tort or contract claim against a state agency has to clear this gate first, and the "manner...directed by law" language is what lets the Legislature impose claim-filing deadlines, damage caps, and administrative exhaustion requirements before a suit against the state can proceed
- Narrower in scope than it looks — it doesn't create a right to sue the state for anything; it just confirms the state _can_ consent to suit, leaving the actual terms to statute

### §6 — English as the official language ★★

- Added by Proposition 63 (November 1986), the "English is the Official Language" initiative
- (a) States English is the "common language" of the U.S. and California, intended to preserve and strengthen it without superseding other constitutional rights
- (b) Declares English the official language of the State of California
- (c) Directs the Legislature to enforce this "by appropriate legislation" and to take all necessary steps to preserve and enhance English's role — and specifically bars the Legislature from passing any law that "diminishes or ignores" that role
- (d) Grants any California resident or business standing to sue the state to enforce the section, with courts of record given jurisdiction to hear such suits
- Largely symbolic in practical effect — federal civil rights law (Title VI, language-access requirements in voting, courts, and public services) constrains how far a state "official language" declaration can actually reach, so §6 has not translated into widespread elimination of bilingual government services
- Worth noting for your political-media-theory interests: a good example of a symbolically potent, practically limited constitutional provision — the kind of clause that does more cultural/rhetorical work than legal work

### §7 — Legislators' Retirement System anti-windfall provision ★

- Added by Proposition 57 (1986)
- Narrow and technical: prevents a former elected officer's retirement allowance (calculated based on the salary of the office they last held) from being inflated by _later_ salary increases to that office after November 5, 1986
- Explicitly framed by its own text as an anti-windfall measure — the People and Legislature declared they didn't intend retirees to get a "dramatic increase" in pension value just because the current officeholder's salary later went up
- Essentially a pension-technical fix; almost never independently litigated outside the retirement-system context it was built for

### §8 — California Citizens Compensation Commission ★★

- Added by Proposition 112 (June 1990), later touched by Proposition 1F (May 2009)
- Creates a 7-member commission, appointed by the Governor, that sets the salary and benefits (medical, dental, insurance) for state officers — the Governor, Lieutenant Governor, Attorney General, Controller, Insurance Commissioner, Secretary of State, Superintendent of Public Instruction, Treasurer, Board of Equalization members, and Legislature members
- Deliberately structured with mixed representation: public members, business-community members, and labor-organization members, with an eye toward geographic, gender, racial, and ethnic diversity
- Bars anyone who was a current or former state officer/employee from serving on the commission — an explicit anti-self-dealing safeguard, since the whole point was to take legislators out of the business of setting their own pay
- Proposition 1F (2009) added the rule that legislators and state officials cannot receive pay increases in years the state budget is in deficit — a fiscally punitive/populist add-on adopted in the aftermath of the 2008–09 budget crisis
- A good example of California using direct democracy to solve a structural conflict-of-interest problem (self-dealing on salary) that the federal system leaves to ordinary legislative self-restraint

### §9 — Surplus state property proceeds ★

- Added by Proposition 60A (2004)
- Directs proceeds from the sale of surplus state property first toward paying down the principal and interest on bonds issued under the 2004 Economic Recovery Bond Act; once those bonds are retired, proceeds flow into the state's Special Fund for Economic Uncertainties
- Explicitly excludes property purchased with Article XIX (transportation) funds or other special-fund money from counting as "surplus" for this purpose
- Narrow fiscal-technical provision tied to a specific early-2000s budget crisis; not something with an ongoing litigation life
