
# Article I: Legislative branch 
## Section 1: Vesting Clause
- "All legislative Powers herein granted shall be vested in a Congress of the United States, which shall consist of a Senate and House of Representatives."
	- "Herein granted" is interpreted as Congress's powers as finite
		- Presidential powers in Article II are "vested in", meaning they get more wiggle room for argument. Congress is more constrained
## Section 2: The House of Representatives
- Qualifications:
	- 2 year terms
	- At least 25 years old
	- A US citizen for at least 7 years
	- - An inhabitant of the state they represent (at the time of the election)
- does NOT fix a specific number of seats put the process of adding them
	- Seats are apportioned among states by population, reapportioned after each census
	- Fixes the minimum representative to one, and it caps it as 1 rep per 20,000 people
		- **Three-fifths clause**
			- Lead to some rlater acist interpretations that slaves would just be counted as three-fifths a person in order to make the math work
				- nullfied by the 14th Ammendment
		- So house could only grow as population grew, not shrink below ratio
		- Later statute, "Permanent Apportionment Act" (1929) capped House at 435 seats
	- technically not against the constitution, but obviously is criticized for limiting it
- The House "shall chuse their speaker and other officers"
	- nothing else about who the speaker is (doesn't even necessarily need to be a House member)
- Sole power of Impeachment
	- The House—and only the house—can impeach
## Section 2: The Senate

- Qualifications
	- 30 years or older
	- citizen for 9 years
	- resident of the state they are a senator for (at time of election)
- 6 year terms
- Two senators per state
	- the "Great compromise" (or Connecticut Compromise) between large states who wanted population-based representation and small states who wanted equal representation
	- Article V: no state shall be deprived of equal Senate representation without its consent
- Federal senators were chosen by state legislatures, not voters directly
	- changed via the 17th Amendment in 1913
- Staggered terms
	- Constitution explicitly divides senators into three groups so that roughly one-third face elections every two years.
		- This was to make the senate more stable
- The Vice President of the United States acts as President of the Senate
	- Which is why the VP is referred to as "Mr. President" while acting in Senate
	- The only votes "unless they be equally divided" 
		- aka only do tie breakers
- Senate tries impeachment
	- "the Senate shall have the sole Power to try all Impeachments"
	- requires two-thirds votes
	- If its a presidental impeachment trial, the chief Justice presides instead of the VP
		- cause you don't want the VP, who is tied to the president, to break a tie

## Section 4: Elections
- Sates control "the Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections" for elections
	- congress can override state rules by law except where senators are chosen
		- 17th ammendement got rid of the exception
- Congress must meet at least once a year
	- originally set for the first Monday in December
		- 20th Ammendment moved it to Jan 3rd
## Section 5: Rules and Proceedings
- Note: text uses "each House", but this refers to BOTH House of Reps and Senate
- Each House judges its own members elections and qualifications
	- can expel a member with two-thirds vote
- A majority constitutes a quorum to do business
- Each house can set its own rules of proceedings and punish members for disorderly behavior
- Each house must keep a journal of its proceedings and publish it 
	- has exceptions for needs of secrecy
	- Textual basis for *Congressional Record*
- Neither House can adjourn for more than 3 days or move to a different location without the other's consent

## Section 6: Compensation and Privileges
- Members get paid "a Compensation... to be ascertained by law"
	- So congress sets its own pay
		- Modified by 27th Amendement
- Privilege from arrest
	- members can't be arrested during sesssions (except for treason, felony, or breach of pease). can't be arrested while traveling to/fron congress
		- Was basically meant to stop executive harassing and leading bogus arrests
- **Speech and Debate Clause**
	- members can't be sued or proescuted for anything they say in legislative debate
- **Incompatibility Clause**
	- a sitting member of Congress cant simultaneously hold another federal office, and can't be appointed to a federal office that was created during their current term (or that had its pay increased in the current term)
		- to prevent congress from creating cushy jobs that members can fill
	- Also states "no Person holding any Office under the United States, shall be a Member of either House during his Continuance in Office"
		- so other offices can't be house/senate members

## Section 7: The Legislative Process
- Important aspect of how congress works.
	- interestingly enough, the process of actually drafting a bull and passing through the house/senate is not stated here. But the process of amendment and veto is 
	- It's very vague on the particulars of the process
- tax bills must start in the *house*, not the senate. 
	- House gets first move on anything that raises federal revenue
	- This was because the house was the only legislature elected by the people who would be taxed initially
	- Senate can amend a revenue bill once the house passes it over
- **The presentment clause**
	- After bill passes both house/senate, it must be presented to the president
	- president signs it = becomes law
	- president vetoes = sent back to the house or senate (whereever it originated) along with written objections
		- mandated to enter objections into its official journal
	- Override attept: the originating house/senate can vote to pass the bill again despite the veto
		- two-thirds agreement then it goes to the other chamber and must also hit two-thirds
			- then it becomes law automatically
	- All override votes are recorded individually (yeas and nays)
	- The 10 day rule. If the president does nothing, doesn't sign or veto, for 10 days (excluding Sundays) while congress is in session, the bill becomes law automatically as if he signed it
		- so the president can't kill legislation by ignoring it
	- Pocket veto exception
		- if congress adjourns before the 10-day window is up, and the president doesn't sign it, the bill dies
- **The Orders, Resolutions, and Votes Clause**
	- "Every Order, Resolution, or Vote to which the Concurrence of the Senate and House of Representatives may be necessary... shall be presented to the President..."
		- anti-loop hole
		- Makes it so congress can't just dodge the entire veto process by labeling something as a joint resolution or concurrent order instead of a bill. 
			- basically, you can just legislate something, say "it's technically not a bill" to get away with law
		- It closes a gap that anything requiring both chamber's agreement gets the same presenment treatment as an actual bill
			- subject to veto
	- There is one explicit exception, votes to adjourn are excluded
		- congress doesn't need president's sign off to adjourn even though that vote goes between both chambers
## Section 8: EnumeratedPowers
- Levy Taxes, duties, imposts, and excises
- Borrow Money on credit of the US
- regulate commerce "among several states" with foreign nations
	- Commerce Clause was expanded upon later
		- greatly expanded on over time
- coin/mint money and regulate its value
- establish post offices and post roads
- issue patents and copyrights ("promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts")
- Establish "an uniform Rule of Naturalization"
	- the federal basis of immigration/citizenship law
		- AKA, why current republican SCOTUS members are fucking nanners for thinking they could just do away with it.
- Declare War/raise and support an army and navy
	- Restriction: no appropriation for the army can last more than 2 years
		- aka no perma standing army
- provide and maintain a navy
- organize, arm, and call forth state militias
- Establish lower federal courts
	- congress decides on how many lower courts exist under SCOTUS
- exercise exclusive legislative authority over the federal capital
	- constitutional basis for DC's non-state status. Federal district
- The "necessary and proper clause" (aka the Elastic Clause)
	- Congress can make laws "necessary and proper" for carrying out its other powers.
	- This is the clause that's been stretched the most over time to justify expanded federal power

## Section 9: Limits on Congress
- congress can't ban the slave trade before 1808
	- was a compromise built in with a 20 year expiration
		- obviously kept going after
- No suspension of habeas corpus except in cases of rebellion or invasion when public saftey requires it
- No bill of attainder 
	- law that punishes a specific person without trial
- No ex post facto laws
	- criminalizing something retroactively and then punishing people
- No tax on exports from any state
	- there's a long history of SCOTUS cases on interstate trade if I recall
- No granting of titles of nobility
	- aka, you cannot accept gifs, titles, or payments from foreign states without Congress's consent
		- **Emoluments Clause**
			- relevant today

# Article II:  Executive Branch

## Section 1: The office itself

### Presidential Office
- "The executive power shall be vested in the President of the United States of America"
	- broad and undefined compared to the congress
	- Leads to much debate about how much power the president has.
- 4 year terms shared with Vice President, elected together
	- No term limits until later amendments
- Qualifications:
	- Natural born citizen (or citizen at the time the Constitution was adopted)
	- 35 years of age
	- resident in the US for 14 years
### Electoral College 
- In Section 1 still, and is the bulk of section 1
- Each state appoints Electors equal to its total Senators + Representatives in whatever manner the state legislature chooses
	- Electors vote for President and Vice-President
		- Each elector got two votes. Whoever in the running had the most votes became President. Whoever got the second most votes became Vice President
		- This was because the framers did not imagine political parties
		- The idea was first place runs the country and second place is the backup/advisor
		- Ties would go to the House of Representatives where each delegation gets one vote
			- Which is what happened in 1800 with Thomas Jefferson vs Aaron Burr
				- the Democrat-Republican part intended Jefferson as the president and Burr as the VP, but this didn't happen
				- Issue is, if the party doesn't control the house but gets a tie, the opposing party can just fuck with the vote
		- Changed with the 12 ammendment (1804)
### Vacancy/Inability 
- Power shall devolve on the Vice President if the president dies, resigns, or is unable to serve
	- It is vague on whether the VP actually *becomes* the president officially or not.
		- When William Henry Harrison died in 1841, this forced a resolution
### Oath of Office
- Sworn oath of the President
	- "I do solemnly swear (or affirm) that I will faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States, and will to the best of my Ability, preserve, protect and defend the Constitution of the United States."
		- Note, you can use "affirm" instead of swear if you do not wish to invoke God (which is what it meant "to swear"). 
			- Refusing Oaths was core practice for Quakers, so this was necessary
			- Essentially, was trying to make this more secular

## Section 2: Powers
### List of Presidential Powers
- Commander in chief of the Army, Navy, and state militias when called into federal service
	- President does NOT have right to declare war
- "may require the Opinion, in writing, of the principal Officer in each of the executive Departments, upon any Subject relating to the Duties of their resecptive Offices"
	- Often called the **Opinion Clause**
	- Translated: *if executive departments exist, the President can ask their heads for written opinions on stuff related to their jobs.*
		- It does not create departments, name them, or gives the ability for the president to create a cabinet
			- it just assumes executive departments will exist somehow
- Pardons and reprieves
	- for federal offenses (not state crimes or impeachments)
- Appointments
	- ambassadors, judges, "Other Officers of the United states"
		- needs Senate confirmation with majority vote
			- can let low level officials be appointed without confirmation if they wish
- Recess appointments
	- President can temporarily fill vacancies when the Senate is not in session without confirmation
		- contested over what counts as "real recess"
## Section 3: Duties
- Give Congress information "The State of the Union... from time to time"
	- doesn't have to be a speech. That's tradition
- Recommend measures to congress
	- can propose legislation but can't introduce or vote on bills
- Can convene Congress for special sessions
- Receieve foreign ambassadors
- "He shall take Care that the Laws be faithfuly executed"
	- **Take Care Clause**
	- Constitutional anchor for the entire modern executive branch and federal bureaucracy
		- EPA, FBI, IRS, CIA, etc.
			- None of these are named explicitly, but are justified under this clause
	- Also has been a justification for Executive Orders
- Commision all officers of the united states
## Section 4: Removal
- Impeachment
	- The President, VP, and civil officers can be removed for "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors"
	- The House impeaches (majority vote), the Senate tries and convicts (two-thirds vote required)

# Article III: Judicial Branch

## Section 1: Judicial Power
- Only one court is constitutionally required: The Supreme Court
	- All other courts were exist because Congress chose to create it
		- Judiciary Act of 1789
- No number of justices is specified
	- 9 justices is arbitrary
		- Comes from Judiciary Act of 1869
		- been as low as 5 and as high as 10
	- Why court packing is possible
- No listed qualifications for judges
- Tenure is "during good Behavior"
	- "good behavior" means they serve "as long as they are not impeached and convicted"
	- don't have mandatory retirement ages or term limits
		- so they serve until the resign or die
	- It also does NOT say they have to be appointed to the same bench the entire time
		- AKA, they don't HAVE to be on SCOTUS, they could, hypothetically, be reappointed. 
			- this has never been done though, but people are suggesting it now as an option
				- Issue is, SCOTUS would have to rule if it is constitutional or not, and obviously they would most likely rule in their own favor of staying on it
- Salary protection
		- Judge's compensation cannot be diminished during their tenure
			- so congress can't punish a judge for an unpopular ruling by cutting their pay

## Section 2: What Courts Can Hear
- defines federal judicial power
### Cases and Controversies
- Cases arise under the constitution, federal laws, and treatise ("federal question" jurisdiction)
- cases affecting ambassadors and other foreign diplomats
- Admiralty and maritime cases
- Cases where the United States is a party
- Disputes between two or more states
- Disputes between a state and a citizen of another state
	- 11th Amendment in 1795 restricted this after *Chrisholm v Georgia*
- Dispute between citizens of different states
	- doesn't usually go to supreme court, but since it can't be resolved in state courts, will go to a federal court
- Disputes involving land grants from different states
- Cases involving a state or its citizens against foreign states/citizens

### Original vs appellate juridiction
- SCOTUS has **original jurisdiction** (cases start there directly, no lower court first) only in two categories
	- cases affecting ambassadors/diplomats, and cases where a state is a party
- SCOTUS has **appellate jurisdiction** (reviews decisions that already went through lower court, and only hears them at congress's discretion) for everything else
	- So congress has some constitutional ability to control what SCOTUS is allowed to appeal
- Jury trial guarantee
	- All crimes (except impeachment) shall be by trial
	- trial must happen where the crime was committed
		- 6th Amendment in Bill of Rights later detailed this more

## Section 3: Treason
- This is the only crime that is explicitly defined in the Constitution
	- This was because the British Crown had a vague definition of treason that they could throw at political dissent
- Categories
	1) Levying war against the US
	2) Giving aid and comfort to enemies
- Note that there is nothing about criticizing the government, opposing policy, or even violent rebellion against a *state* (says nothing about rebellion against the federal union)
- Conviction of Treason requires either
	- testimony of two witnesses to the same overt act
		- more strict than most crimes, used as protection over treason being used as a political weapon
	- Confession in open court
- Punishment limits
	- congress sets the punishment except for "Corruption of Blood" and most forfeiture beyond convicted person's own lifetime
		- so treason can't punish the family of the traitor
			- This was in contrast to the English practice where the whole family line could be punished

### Notably Absent (built later, not in the Constitution)
- judicial review
	- discussed in *Marbury v Madison* (1803)
	- So it was never an intended check/balance for SCOTUS to review laws and deem them constitutional
- Lower federal courts' structure
	- district courts, circuit of courts of appeals, etc.
		- set in stone by Judiciary act of 1891 and later reforms
- Judicial qualifications 
	- lawyer, bar membership, etc.
	- This is custom, it is not a legal requirement that a judge have passed the bar
- The court chooses its own case (**certiorari**)
	- A discretionary process where SCOTUS picks which appeals to hear
	- Was made mainly due to the "judges' bill" (**Judiciary Act of 1925**)
- the number of SCOTUS justices 

# Article IV: States' Relations to Other States and the Federal Government

## Section 1: Full Faith and Credit
- forces states to honor each other's official acts
	- court judgments
	- marriage licenses
	- adoption decrees
	- corporate charters
	- public records
	- etc.
- Congress can pass laws about how states prove and give effect to each other's records
	- Defense of Marriage Act (1996)
		- attempt to make states able refuse to recognize same-sex marriages performed elsewhere
			- *Obergefell v Hodges* (2015) required all states to recognize same-sex marriage nationally on 14th amendment grounds
- A state doesn't necessarily have to adopt another state's entire legal framework, just specific judicial outcomes and records

## Section 2: Interstate Citizen Rights
- Privileges and immunities
	- stops states from treating out-of-state visitors like second class citizens
		- they can own property, engage in trade, travel through
		- Not every state benefit must be shared (like university tuition)
	- Different from Privileges or Immunities Clause of 14th Amendment
- Extradition
	- If someone commits crime in one state and flees to another, the first state can demand the other state to send the criminal back
		- Usually fine, but a few accute moments in 1800s where governors refused to comply
			- SCOTUS ruled that this part of the Constitution couldn't be judicially forced 
- Fugitive Slave clause
	- Allows people in service of a state that tries to escape to another state to be returned to the original state
		- never uses the word "slave"
			- "Person held to Service or Labor" = slave in practice
		- Interesting how this differs in quality from above
	- Gave justification to **Fugitive Slave Acts of 1793 and 1850**
	- 13th Amendment (1865) nullified this in effect
		- though this clause still exists in the OG constitution, since the original document is not rewritten

## Section 3: New States and Federal Property
- Congress alone can decide whether or not to admit a news state
- a new state cannot be carved out of an existing one or formed by merging states WITHOUT consent of congress and the states
	- Issue was West Virginia in 1863
		- Virginia wouldn't consent, but it was deemed consent came from a Union-recognized ruling. Virgina's refusal wasn't considered legitimate, and only West Virginia's party was considered the legitimate say
			- Constitutionally shaky
- Property clause
	- gives congress the ability to own, manage, and restrict federal land
		- national parks, federal buildings, military bases, etc.

# Section 4: Guarantee Clause
1) Every state gets a Republican From of Government
	- states can't have dictatorships
2) Federal protection against invasion
	- federal government will intervene in case of foreign invasion
3) Federal government will help against insurrection/violence within state
	- only if state's legislature requests it
		- Often leads to questions whether or not National Guard being sent into a state often brings question of state's consent
			- Like Trump sending National Guard into CA during ICE riots
				- Newsom didn't consent and legally the federal troops were kicked out

# Article V: Amendments
- This article is just one paragraph
- Two ways to propose an amendment
	1) Congressional route
		- 2/3 of both house and senate vote to propose an amendment
			- ALL amendments in history have gone this route
	2) Convention route
		- 2/3 of state legislatures (34 out of 50 states) formally apply to congress to call a constitutional convention for the purpose of proposing amendments. 
			- Congress is obligated to call one
			- Never used, but not for lack of trying (have come close before)
			- Nobody actually knows how this would work
- Two ways to ratify an amendment
	1) state legislature route
		- 3/4 of state legislatures (38/50) approve it
	2) state convention route
		- 3/4 of states hold special ratifying conventions
	- Congress gets to pick which ratification method applies for proposed amendments
		- In practice, 26 out of 27 amendments were ratified via state legislatures
			- Only the 21st Amendment (repealing prohibition) congress chose the state-convention method because legislatures were seen as vulnerable to pressure from lobbying groups
- Explicitly stated Article I, Section 9 of the Constitution could not be amended until 1808
	- let international slave trade continue until 1808
- **entrenchment clause** Explicitly states that the only thing that can't be amended is that each state gets 2 senate members without consent of the state
	- hypothetically, you could amend this clause out of the constitution, then amend the senate directly


# Article VI: Federal Government Binds
- structured with clauses not sections
- debts owed by the unofficial government before the constitution are still owed after the constitution is ratified
- **Supremacy Clause**: three things are named as supreme law
	1) The Constitution
	2) Federal laws made "in pursuance" of the constitution (aka, constitutionally approved laws)
	3) treaties 
	- state judges are bound by this
	- Basis of federal preemption doctrine
		- aka, whether federal standard set a ceiling or a floor that states can go beyond
		- Was used as basis for why State's couldn't ignore the federal government during civil war
- All federal officials, state legislators, state executive officers, state judges are required to swear an oath to support the federal constitution
	- Can also be "affirm" an oath (swearing = god, Quakers couldn't swear on God's name). So it was less religious
		- State governments could still require religious based texts

# Article VII: Closing
- A single sentence
	- "The Ratification of the Conventions of nine States, shall be sufficient for the Establishment of this Constitution between the States so ratifying the Same."
		- only takes 9 of 13 states to ratify it
			- previous Articles of Confederation required all 13 states, and this caused a lot of dysfunction since one state could block indefinitely (like Rhode Island)
		- It also meant that only the states that ratify it would be bounded to it (so 9 would be bound, but if 4 didn't, they wouldn't be bound)
			- North Carolina and Rhode Island didn't do that until 1 and 2 years later respectively
		- Had conventions not state legislatures
