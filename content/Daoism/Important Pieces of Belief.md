## Zhengyi Daoism (Orthodox Unity)


## Practices

Focuses on "physical cultivation" and self-commitment to Wu Wei (effortless living). One must attune their patterns to that of the Dao.

The body is no longer a means to living in harmony; it becomes the universe itself. 

Self-cultivation practice focus on transforming the mind-heart together with "bodily subtances and energies" like **jing** or **qi** to connect them to the natural power and universal forces

There are communal rituals too.

Taoism teaches detachment in contrast to Confucian humanism, but Taoism is not misanthropic or nihilistic. Humans are an important class of things in the world. But they are not especially important compared to other aspects (they though the same for their deities too).

Daoism includes a plethora of subjects: aesthetics, art, dietetics, ethics, health, longevity, meditation, ritual, seasonal attunement, scripture study, etc.

Health is a big part of it. There are many ancient Taoist alchemical books of medicine and such.

Mountains are sacred spaces for practice. It may include yunyou (cloud wandering). This is why monasteries were often in mountains.

## The Nine Practices (Nine Virtues)

[Sourced from wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism#Practices)

Taught in Celestial Masters School, drawn from sources, mainly the Tao Te Ching

1) **Nonaction (Wu Wei)**: Acting in a way that does not force outcomes, responding to situations with minimal contrivance so things unfold in accordance with the Dao.
2. **Softness and weakness (róuruò):** Valuing flexibility, humility, and yielding strength, on the model of water, which overcomes hardness by not resisting it head-on.
3. **Guarding the feminine (shǒucí):** “Holding to the receptive” by maintaining a quiet, nurturing, non-dominating posture that preserves inner vitality and avoids aggressive display.
4. **Being nameless (wúmíng):** Not fixating on labels, status, or reputations, and returning to the prior-to-concepts simplicity from which distinctions arise.
5. **Clarity and stillness (qīngjìng):** Cultivating mental and energetic quiet so perception becomes clear and one can align with the natural order without agitation.
6. **Being adept (zhūshàn):** Developing broad skill in “the goods” (virtues/beneficial actions), so one’s conduct is reliably helpful, timely, and appropriate.
7. **Being desireless (wúyù):** Reducing grasping and craving that distort judgment, so action is guided by what is fitting rather than what the ego wants.
8. **Knowing how to stop and be content (zhī zhǐzú):** Recognizing sufficiency (setting limits and resting satisfied) so ambition doesn’t become compulsive and destructive.
9. **Yielding and withdrawing (tuīràng):** Stepping back, giving way, and letting others take precedence when appropriate, reducing conflict and maintaining harmony.
